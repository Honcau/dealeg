Webflow's pricing confuses people more than any other builder's, and it isn't because the numbers are hidden — they're published. It's because there are **two separate plan types that do different things**, and most newcomers assume there's one. Here's the structure, the traps, and how to pay the least for what you actually need.

## The two-plan structure

**Site plans** are per website. You buy one for each site you want to publish on a custom domain. This is the bill most people think of as "Webflow."

**Workspace plans** are per team and per seat. They govern how many unhosted projects you can have, whether you get code export, how many people can edit, and access to some professional features.

You often need **both**. A freelancer publishing three client sites pays three Site plans plus a Workspace plan. That surprise — "wait, I'm paying twice?" — is the single most common complaint about Webflow's pricing, and it's a fair one.

## Site plans, in plain terms

- **Free / staging** — build anything, publish only to a `yourname.webflow.io` subdomain. Genuinely useful for learning and prototypes. No custom domain.
- **Basic** — custom domain, no CMS. Fine for a static brochure or landing page.
- **CMS** — adds Collections (blog, portfolio, dynamic content), more form submissions, a few content editors. **This is where most real projects land.**
- **Business** — higher limits, faster hosting tier, more editors. For high-traffic marketing sites.
- **Ecommerce** — separate tier on top, with transaction fees on the lowest tier.

Annual billing is meaningfully cheaper than monthly — expect roughly a 20%+ saving. If you're confident about the project, annual is the obvious call.

## Where the real ceilings are

The pricing page lists limits; these are the ones that actually bite:

**CMS item caps.** Each Site plan allows a fixed number of CMS items. A blog that publishes weekly takes years to hit it. A recipe site, a job board, or a product catalogue hits it fast — and the fix is a more expensive plan, not a smarter architecture.

**Collections per site.** There's a cap on how many Collections you can define. Complex content models run out of room.

**Form submissions.** Capped per month and per plan. High-volume lead-gen sites need a higher tier or an external form service.

**Editors/seats.** Adding people who can edit content pushes you up plans. This is the one agencies underestimate.

## The trap: paying for staging you don't need

The most common way people overpay: buying a Site plan for a project that isn't launched yet. You don't need one. Build unlimited projects on the free tier and publish to the `.webflow.io` staging domain for as long as you like. **Only buy the Site plan when you attach a real domain.**

Corollary: if you're pausing a client project, you can downgrade or cancel that site's plan and the project stays in your workspace. You're not obliged to keep paying for a site that's off.

## How Webflow compares on cost

Honestly: **it's expensive for simple sites and reasonable for complex ones.**

A basic brochure site costs meaningfully more on Webflow than Squarespace, and Squarespace will get you there faster. That comparison is not flattering.

A custom-designed marketing site with a blog, where the WordPress alternative is hosting plus a premium page builder plus a caching plugin plus a security plugin plus the hours you'll spend on updates — the gap narrows a lot, and Webflow may come out ahead once your time is priced honestly.

Ecommerce is where the value proposition is weakest: Shopify does that job better for similar money.

## Cutting the bill legitimately

- **Pay annually** — the biggest single lever, roughly a fifth off.
- **Buy the right tier, not the safe one.** Start on CMS; move to Business only when a limit actually stops you.
- **Don't buy Site plans for unlaunched work** — staging is free.
- **Consolidate editors.** Seats are a real cost; not everyone needs edit access.
- **Check for a current promotion** before committing. Webflow discounts run often enough — especially around annual billing and seasonal sales — that paying list price on your first year is avoidable.

## The honest verdict

Webflow's pricing is transparent but structurally awkward: two plan types, real limits, and a bill that grows with sites and seats rather than staying flat. It is not the cheap option and it doesn't pretend to be.

It's fair value **if** design quality is a competitive asset and you're replacing a maintained WordPress stack. It's poor value **if** you want a simple site cheaply — Squarespace or a Hostinger builder plan will serve you better for less.

Do the honest sum before you commit: your Site plan tier, plus a Workspace seat if you need one, plus any ecommerce tier, billed annually. Compare that to the real total cost of the alternative including your time. Then buy on a promotion.
