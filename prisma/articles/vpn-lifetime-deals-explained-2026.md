"Lifetime VPN — $39, one payment, forever." You've seen the ad. It shows up on deal sites, in Black Friday roundups, and in the sidebar of every tech blog. The offer is real in the sense that you can pay the money. Whether you get a lifetime of VPN is a different question. Here's the honest arithmetic.

## The problem is the business model, not the marketing

Running a VPN costs money every month, forever: servers, bandwidth, staff, audits, app development. Bandwidth in particular scales with use — a customer who streams 200GB a month costs the provider real money that month, and every month after.

Now sell that customer a $39 one-off payment for unlimited service for life. The revenue arrives once. The cost recurs indefinitely. There is no version of that spreadsheet that works, unless one of these is true:

- **The "lifetime" isn't yours — it's the product's.** Terms usually define lifetime as the lifetime of the service, not the customer. If the plan is discontinued, so is your access. That's not a loophole; it's often written down.
- **They're banking on you not using it.** Like gym memberships. Most buyers stop using a VPN within a year; the provider pockets the $39 and carries almost no cost.
- **New customers subsidise old ones.** This works until growth slows. Then it doesn't.
- **You're the product.** Some free and lifetime VPNs have historically monetised through the traffic itself — logging, injecting, or selling bandwidth. This is the genuinely dangerous version.

## What actually happens to lifetime VPN buyers

The pattern is well documented across a decade of these offers:

**Service quietly degrades.** Lifetime customers get deprioritised — slower servers, fewer locations, older apps. You still have access, technically.

**The plan is "migrated."** The provider restructures, and lifetime accounts are converted to a limited tier or given a discount on the new subscription. Your lifetime becomes a coupon.

**The reseller vanishes.** Many lifetime deals are sold through third-party deal platforms, not the VPN itself. When the arrangement ends, support becomes a maze.

**The company disappears.** Small providers exit. Your lifetime ends with theirs.

The tell is simple: the credible providers — the ones with independent audits and real engineering teams — **do not sell lifetime plans**. NordVPN doesn't. Surfshark doesn't. Mullvad charges a flat €5 every month and refuses to discount at all. That's not a coincidence. They price to cover recurring costs because the costs recur.

## The one honest exception

Occasionally a legitimate provider runs a genuinely long prepay — three or five years at a deep discount. That's not a lifetime deal; it's a bulk purchase with a defined end date, and the maths works because the term is finite.

Those can be good value if you're confident about the provider. The risk is different but real: you're betting five years on a company's trustworthiness, and the VPN market changes fast. A provider that's excellent today can be acquired by someone you'd rather not trust. Three years is a reasonable ceiling for most people.

## What to buy instead

**If you want the lowest honest cost:** a discounted multi-year plan from an audited provider — Surfshark, PureVPN, or similar — with a calendar reminder set for the renewal date. That reminder is the whole trick. The renewal, not the promo, is where VPN money is actually lost.

**If you want to never think about it:** Mullvad's flat €5/month. No promo, no renewal cliff, no upsell, cancel any time. Over five years it often beats a "cheap" plan that renews at triple.

**If you want it free and safe:** use the free tier of a provider with a real business model (Proton VPN's free tier is the standard answer). Limited, slow, honest. Nobody is monetising your traffic to pay for it.

## The honest verdict

Lifetime VPN deals aren't universally a scam, but they are universally a bet — you're wagering that the provider keeps honouring an unprofitable promise for as long as you want service. Sometimes you win a couple of years. You will not win a lifetime.

The rule of thumb: **if the price doesn't cover the cost of serving you, you're not the customer.** For $39, nobody is going to carry your bandwidth for a decade. Spend the same money on a discounted two- or three-year plan from a provider that has been audited, set the reminder, and re-evaluate when it expires. That's boring advice, and it's the advice that leaves you with a working VPN in 2030.
